December guide: what Goldman's $100 billion "robot selling" warning means for investors

Von Analyst Ledger research desk · 2026-11-28 · 5 Min. Lesezeit

Goldman Sachs expects rules-based funds to sell about $100 billion of global stocks this week, after $70 billion last week, then to slow and buy back modestly in early December. We put readers' questions to our research desk: is it real, does it mean higher prices in December, and what could go wrong.

An editorial in question-and-answer form. The questions are the ones our readers asked after a widely shared post on 27 November; the answers come from the Analyst Ledger research desk.

The warning in one paragraph

In a client note dated Wednesday 25 November, Goldman Sachs' trading desk, led by Paul Leyzerovich, estimated that systematic funds sold about $70 billion of global stocks in the previous week and could sell roughly $100 billion more in the week ahead. Its baseline is that the selling then stabilises and turns into small purchases in early December. The note landed after a sharp tech-led fall: according to reports of the note, the S&P 500 lost 8.6% in the week to 20 November, its worst week in 19 months, and the Nasdaq 100 was close to 20% below its 6 October record.

Q: Is the "$100 billion automated sell-off" story true?

A: The numbers are reported accurately. Several outlets that saw or summarised the note give the same figures: about $70 billion sold last week, about $100 billion forecast for this week, and a turn to modest buying in early December. That makes the "$170 billion in two weeks" total correct.

What the viral version leaves out matters more than what it says:

  • It is a forecast from a model, not a done deal. The funds involved follow fixed rules based on price trends and volatility. Goldman estimates what those rules will do; if markets move differently, the real number changes.
  • It is global. Goldman's desk said most of the remaining systematic risk is outside the US, and that much of the US selling has already happened. Trend-following funds (CTAs) have already moved to a net short position of about $19 billion in US stocks, after selling about $57 billion over the past month.
  • Different funds, one headline. The $100 billion bundles trend-followers with volatility-control funds, which cut stock exposure when markets get jumpy. The latter have trimmed around $400 billion of equities since mid-October, according to reports of the note.

Q: Should investors be alarmed?

A: Alert, not alarmed. Goldman's desk publishes flow estimates like this most weeks, and figures of this size are not unusual. In March 2026, for example, it estimated about $70 billion of selling for the following week and close to $100 billion over the following month. Selling of this kind is spread across many markets and several days, and it often gets absorbed without a crash.

The useful part of the note is the timing: it tells you when mechanical selling pressure is likely to be heaviest (this week, especially in tech) and when it should fade (early December).

Q: Does that mean prices will be higher in December?

A: Not necessarily. Goldman is forecasting that the selling stops, not that prices rise. When these funds stop selling, a source of pressure disappears; the modest buying expected afterwards is only a small push. And these funds are only one group of traders. December prices will also depend on:

  • The Federal Reserve. The last rate decision of the year is due on Wednesday 9 December, after a two-day meeting, with new economic projections.
  • Inflation and jobs data released in early December.
  • News on AI and tech spending, which drove the selling that began in mid-October.
  • Year-end portfolio moves by large investors.

There is also a feedback loop. These funds follow price trends. If prices keep falling, the same models signal more caution, not buying. Goldman's own note says further falls would reduce the early-December buying, although it does not expect heavy selling to resume, because so much has already been sold.

Q: So in theory, December stabilises and prices drift up into year-end?

A: That is a reasonable base case, and many traders work with something like it. It only holds if nothing new shakes the market.

Why it could happen:

  • The automatic selling runs its course, and Goldman's models point to small purchases from early December.
  • December has historically been one of the stronger months for US stocks. The so-called Santa Claus rally, the last five trading days of the year plus the first two of January, has been positive more often than not.
  • Fund managers who are behind their benchmarks sometimes buy before year-end so their annual reports look better.

What could break it:

  • A surprise from the Fed on 9 December, the biggest single scheduled risk.
  • Hotter-than-expected inflation or jobs figures.
  • More bad news from AI and big tech, where this selling started.
  • A shock nobody is modelling: geopolitics, a bank problem, an oil spike.

History is a reminder that seasonality is a tendency, not a rule: December 2018 was one of the worst Decembers for US stocks in decades.

Analysis: a December checklist for investors

  1. This week (30 November to 4 December): expect extra selling pressure, mostly outside the US and in tech-heavy indexes. Large swings in either direction are possible while the systematic funds finish adjusting.
  2. Early December: watch whether the selling actually fades. A calmer VIX (reported at about 24 on Friday, down from above 30 before Thanksgiving) would support Goldman's baseline.
  3. 8 to 9 December: the Fed decision and projections. This is the event most likely to change the picture for the rest of the month.
  4. Mid to late December: if the first three steps go smoothly, the seasonal tailwind and year-end buying can take over. If they don't, the same funds can turn back into sellers.
  5. For options users: a slow recovery usually comes with falling volatility. That makes options cheaper to buy and pays less for selling them; the opposite holds if the selling resumes.

The bottom line

The Goldman note is real and the figures are reported correctly. It describes mechanical, global selling that may weigh on stocks, and on tech in particular, for a few more days, with Goldman expecting it to ease in early December. That removes a headwind; it does not guarantee a rally. A calm, slowly rising December is plausible, perhaps the most likely path, but it depends on the Fed, the data and AI news cooperating. We will update this guide if Goldman's estimates change materially.
